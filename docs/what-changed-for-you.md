# What changed about your numbers

Ledger used to answer the same question in several different ways. "How much did I
spend in June?" gave one answer on the Overview, a different one on the chart below it,
and a third on your budget cards — because each part of the app was working it out on
its own, with slightly different ideas about what counts as spending.

That's now fixed. Every figure comes from one set of rules. The side effect is that a
lot of your numbers have moved, some of them by a lot, and this explains what moved and
why.

**Nothing about your actual money has changed.** No transaction was edited or deleted,
no balance is different. Only the way the app adds things up has changed.

---

## Your spending totals will look lower — often much lower

This is the big one, and it comes down to four things that used to be counted as
spending and no longer are.

**Moving your own money isn't spending.** If you transfer $500 from checking into
savings, the old app counted $500 of spending — and, on some screens, $500 of income
as well. You didn't spend anything; you moved it. Transfers between your own accounts
now drop out of both.

**Paying your credit card bill isn't spending.** The spending happened when you bought
things on the card, and those purchases were already counted. Counting the bill as well
counted the same money twice. A card payment now counts as neither spending nor income.

**Money people send you back isn't income.** When a friend repays you for dinner, that
isn't earnings — it's your own money coming back. It now reduces what you paid out
rather than inflating what you earned.

**Refunds now count properly.** A refund reduces what you spent, in the category you
spent it in. Previously it either did nothing or, on some screens, quietly looked like
income.

Some months will now show a very small spending bar where they used to show a tall one.
That usually means the month's activity was mostly transfers or a card payment — money
moving around rather than money leaving.

## Cash withdrawals still count as spending

Taking $200 out of a cash machine looks, to the bank, a lot like a transfer — your bank
is named on both sides. It isn't one: that money left and got spent. It still counts.

## "Payments to people" is now its own line

Venmo, Zelle, Cash App and the like get their own line, showing both directions: what
you sent and what came back. If people send you back more than you paid out in a month,
the extra shows as money in that we couldn't identify, rather than being counted as
income. It's shown rather than hidden, so you can see it and tell us if we've got it
wrong.

## Your savings rate may sometimes show a dash

If your income in a given month is unusually low, the savings rate stops being
meaningful — a small income under a normal month's spending produces a wild percentage
that tells you nothing. When that happens you'll see "—" instead of a number.

The threshold adapts to you: it's a quarter of your typical recent income, not a fixed
figure, so it fits whatever normal looks like for you. The rate itself is never fudged
or capped — if you genuinely spent 40% more than you earned, it says so.

## Budgets now follow your month, not the calendar's

If you've set your month to start on a day other than the 1st, budgets now use that.
Before, a budget reset on the 1st even if your month started on the 10th, so the numbers
on your budget cards covered a different stretch of time than everything else. Pace is
measured the same way: on the 12th of a month that starts on the 10th, you're two days
in, not twelve.

## Pending transactions count everywhere

A card payment you made yesterday that hasn't fully settled now counts on every screen.
Previously some screens included it and some didn't, so a total could change slightly
overnight for no reason you could see.

## A category can now show a negative number

If you got more back in refunds in a category than you spent in it, that category shows
a negative number. That's real — it's what happened. The old app clipped it to zero,
which hid money coming back to you.

You may also see a line called **Unallocated refunds**. That's a refund we're confident
is a refund, but not confident enough about which category it belongs to. Rather than
guess and put it in the wrong place, it reduces your total spending and sits on its own
line.

---

## If a number looks wrong

It should be explainable, and we'd rather hear about it than have you quietly distrust
the app. Every transaction now has a recorded reason for how it was counted, so a
specific example — "this $60 should be spending and it isn't" — can be traced to the
rule that decided it and fixed if the rule is wrong.

Two judgement calls worth knowing about, because they're the ones most likely to be
wrong for you:

- If you pay off a credit card that isn't connected to Ledger, that payment counts as
  spending, because it's the only trace of that card's purchases we can see.
- If you transfer money to a savings account that isn't connected, it counts as saved
  rather than spent — which means it's money we know left your account but didn't treat
  as spending.

Connecting those accounts makes both cases exact rather than inferred.
